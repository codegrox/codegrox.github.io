Image Warping and Mosaicing

Part A of [Auto]Stitching Photo Mosaics: recovering homographies from point correspondences, inverse warping with two interpolation methods, rectifying planes, blending photos into mosaics, and a 200° cylindrical panorama.

Result snapshot

Every homography on this page comes from my own least-squares solver, fit to 12 correspondences per pair to under 1.2 px. Rectification recovers the real proportions of a rectangle from the phone's focal length. The three mosaics use gain compensation for exposures I did not lock, plus a two-level blend that keeps detail sharp without ghosting. For the bells & whistles, nine photos become a {{w_span}}° cylindrical panorama, sampled directly from the original photos with camera rotations fitted to every point.

Part A.1: Shoot and Digitize Pictures

All photos are from a Pixel 10 Pro on its main 24 mm-equivalent lens (no ultra-wide, which would add barrel distortion), shot at 4080×3072 and stored here at 2048 px on the long side. For every set I stood in one place and turned the phone around its lens, so that neighbouring photos differ only by a camera rotation and are related exactly by a homography. Neighbouring frames overlap by roughly 40–70%. I did not lock exposure, which shows up later as brightness differences that the blending has to absorb.

More sets

I used every remaining sequence that had real overlap and was shot from one spot. All of them are used below: six as planar mosaics and three as cylindrical panoramas. I left out only a single photo with no partner, a pair that mixes the ultra-wide and main lenses, near-duplicate frames, and three close-up sets with strong parallax: a relief wall, a pay station, and the hallway, which only supplied the fire-cabinet rectification.

Part A.2: Recover Homographies

A homography maps a point \(p = (x, y, 1)\) in one photo to \(p' \sim Hp\) in the other. Fixing \(h_{33} = 1\) leaves 8 unknowns, and every correspondence \((x, y) \to (u, v)\) gives two linear equations once the perspective divide is multiplied out:

\[\begin{aligned} h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13} - h_{31}xu - h_{32}yu &= u \\ h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23} - h_{31}xv - h_{32}yv &= v \end{aligned}\]

Four correspondences determine \(H\) exactly, but a couple of pixels of error then swing it a lot, so I use 12 per pair and solve the overdetermined \(2n \times 8\) system \(Ah = b\) by least squares. One numerical detail matters here. With raw pixel coordinates, \(A\) mixes entries around \(10^6\) (the \(xu\) terms) with 1s, and its condition number is {{cond_raw}} for the theatre pair. Following Hartley, I first move each point set's centroid to the origin and scale its mean distance to \(\sqrt 2\). That brings the condition number down to {{cond_norm}}. I then solve and undo the normalization: \(H = T_2^{-1} \tilde H T_1\).

Correspondences

Points have to sit on sharp, unambiguous features: window and sign corners, letters, roof edges. A point on a plain wall or in the sky can slide without anyone noticing. I used a small helper (tests/autocorr.py, not part of the graded code) to propose strong corners and match each one in the neighbouring photo. It keeps a point only if its match is clearly unique and agrees with a single homography fitted to all candidates, which throws out moving cars, and then spreads the final points over the overlap. I checked every pair by eye before using it.

Part A.3: Warp the Images

Both warps are inverse warps. I first push the four image corners through \(H\) to get the output's bounding box. Then, for every output pixel \(p'\), I compute where it came from, \(p = H^{-1}p'\), and sample the source there. Going backwards guarantees exactly one value per output pixel, so there are no holes. Pixel centers sit at integer coordinates, so \((0, 0)\) is the center of the first pixel. Locations that fall outside the source get alpha 0, and that alpha mask later drives the blending. Everything is vectorized over row chunks, so warping a 2048 px photo takes well under a second.

Rectification

To test the warps before building mosaics, I rectified three rectangles photographed at an angle, plus a fourth blind test against a reference photo (below). Corners alone don't give the target shape, because I don't know how big the real rectangle is. But the phone's focal length is known ({{focal_px}} px from EXIF), and with \(K\) known the homography from a unit square to the photographed quadrilateral factors as \(K^{-1}H \propto [\,r_1\; r_2\; t\,]\). The rectangle's true aspect ratio is then \(h/w = \lVert K^{-1}h_2 \rVert / \lVert K^{-1}h_1 \rVert\). This gives {{aspect_windows}} for the window block, {{aspect_cabinet}} for the cabinet glass, and {{aspect_marquee}} for the theatre marquee. The rectified windows come out square and evenly spaced, which independently confirms the computed ratio.

Nearest neighbour vs. bilinear

{{rect_rows}}
RectificationOutputNearestBilinearBilinear cost

Trade-off. Nearest neighbour does one lookup per output pixel. Bilinear does four lookups plus three weighted sums, and measured {{slow_lo}}–{{slow_hi}}× slower here. The quality difference shows wherever the warp stretches or rotates the image: nearest copies whole source pixels, so diagonal edges turn into stair-steps and enlarged areas look blocky. Bilinear averages neighbours, which is slightly softer but continuous. For the mosaics I use bilinear throughout: the extra cost is a fraction of a second per photo, and blocky seams would be far more visible than slight softness.

Blind test: rectify, then compare with a straight-on photo

The examples above are judged by eye. For this one I also took a straight-on reference photo, so the result can be checked with numbers. The subject is a relief sculpture on a shaded wall, photographed at a steep angle. The relief has no rectangular border, but its wall does. The building's corner edge and the doorway's left edge are vertical, and the floor line and the doorway's top edge are horizontal, all on the same plane. Three of the rectangle's corners are physical points: the building corner at the floor, and the doorway's top-left and bottom-left corners. The fourth is where the corner edge meets the doorway's top edge, extended. From these corners and the focal length, the wall rectangle's height/width is {{rel_aspect}}. The reference photo is not used anywhere in the rectification.

To measure the match, I placed the blind result onto the reference with a similarity seeded from 8 landmark pairs (heads, hands, feet). I then refined {{rel_pts}} strong points on the relief by local patch matching. A perfect rectification would leave only a similarity between the two, so the question is how much a richer transform improves the fit:

Almost all of the remaining error is affine: a {{rel_aniso}}% difference in stretch between two directions, with essentially no rotation ({{rel_rot}}°). Allowing perspective on top barely helps ({{rel_aff}} → {{rel_hom}} px), so no measurable perspective is left. In other words, the blind rectification got the plane's orientation right and is about 5% off in proportions. That is about what to expect from four hand-read corners and a focal length rounded to the nearest millimetre. The last few pixels, about {{rel_pct}}% of the {{rel_w}}×{{rel_h}} px relief, most likely come from the relief's own depth, since it is sculpted, not flat, and was seen from two very different angles.

Rectification also cannot recover detail the photo never captured. At the relief, the angled photo recorded only {{rel_sqx}} source pixels per output pixel horizontally ({{rel_sqy}} vertically). The rectified relief is therefore about {{rel_up}}× upsampled sideways: the geometry is right, but it is visibly softer than the straight-on reference.

Part A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I build every mosaic in one shot, in five steps:

  1. Chain to a reference. The middle photo stays unwarped. Each neighbour homography maps photo \(k\) into photo \(k+1\), so composing them outward gives every photo's homography into the reference.
  2. Size the canvas as the union of all warped bounding boxes, then warp each photo once, at full resolution, into its own box inside that canvas.
  3. Gain compensation. I did not lock exposure, so neighbouring photos differ in brightness. On a quarter-size preview I measure each overlap's mean color and solve for one gain per photo and channel (Brown & Lowe's formulation), so that overlaps agree while every gain stays near 1. Without that prior, "all gains = 0" would trivially make every overlap agree.
  4. Feathering. Each photo's weight is its distance to the edge of its own footprint (the distance transform, MATLAB's bwdist), so each pixel trusts the photo whose center it is closest to.
  5. Two-level blend (a 2-level Laplacian pyramid). Feathering alone averages misaligned fine detail into faint double edges (ghosting). The spec suggests a 2-level pyramid for exactly this, so I split each photo into a blurred low band (Gaussian, σ = 4 px) and a detail band (photo minus low band). The low band is feathered, which gives a smooth transition in color and exposure. The detail band is taken from whichever photo has the largest weight at that pixel, so edges stay single and sharp.

When a plane is the wrong surface

My Golden Gate set shows the limit of a planar mosaic. Its third photo is tilted about 40° up toward a flag. Projected onto the middle photo's plane, everything far from the center is stretched enormously: the flag dominates and the bridge shrinks into a corner. The homographies are correct; the plane simply cannot hold a wide field of view without stretching. Dropping the tilted frame fixes it: the 2-photo Golden Gate pan in the gallery above is clean. The cylindrical projection below fixes it properly, for any width.

Part A.5: Bells & Whistles: Cylindrical and Spherical Mosaics

A plane cannot hold a wide sweep: at 90° from the reference, the planar projection goes to infinity. My nine-photo sweep turns {{w_turn}}° and covers {{w_span}}° in total. On a cylinder (or sphere) around the camera, that's no problem. My pipeline, in five steps:

  1. Rotations from homographies. For a camera that only rotates, \(H = K R K^{-1}\). Projecting \(K^{-1}HK\) onto the nearest rotation matrix (via SVD) is the textbook shortcut, but it only minimizes an algebraic error. On my photos it reproduced the points only to {{w_svd_lo}}–{{w_svd_hi}} px.
  2. Fit the rotations in pixels. Instead I fit all eight neighbour rotations and one shared focal length jointly to the clicked points, with robust least squares (a small bundle adjustment). Seven of the eight pairs now fit to {{w_fit_lo}}–{{w_fit_hi7}} px (the last one to {{w_fit_max}} px). The fitted focal length is {{w_ffit}} px, {{w_fratio}}× the EXIF value of {{w_fexif}} px. That's how I picked the radius: by measurement, and it agrees with the phone.
  3. Straighten. When you turn in place, every camera's x-axis lies in the horizontal plane, so "up" is the direction most perpendicular to all of them. The middle photo was tilted {{w_tilt}}° from level; rotating the cylinder's axis onto the true up direction removes the wavy horizon.
  4. Inverse-sample from the originals. For every output pixel I compute its viewing ray on the surface, rotate it into each camera, project with \(K\), and sample that original photo bilinearly. No photo is warped twice, so the panorama keeps the sources' full resolution.
  5. Blend with the same gains and two-level blend as the planar mosaics.

What I Learned

The most important lesson was that the error you minimize matters as much as the model. Every step here has a cheap algebraic version and a geometric one. Solving \(Ah = b\) on raw pixels works, but normalizing first changes the condition number by orders of magnitude. Rounding \(K^{-1}HK\) to a rotation is one line of code, but it was off by up to {{w_svd_hi}} px, while fitting the same rotations to the actual points brought them to about 1–2 px. Both versions "work"; only one is accurate.

The second lesson was that bugs in blending are geometric, too. My first feathered mosaic had a sharp rectangular seam, because the unwarped reference photo fills its whole box and the distance transform never reached zero at its edge. Padding the mask by one pixel fixed it. That was a one-line fix, and I only found it because I looked at a zoomed crop rather than the full mosaic.
